# Cardiovascular Changes and Monitoring in Spaceflight

> ASTRAHEALTH educational note (team-authored summary for a hackathon prototype). Not an official NASA document.

## Fluid shifts in microgravity
In weightlessness, body fluids shift toward the head and upper body. The cardiovascular system adapts to this new distribution over days to weeks. Facial puffiness and nasal congestion are commonly described early in flight.

## Cardiovascular deconditioning
Without the normal pull of gravity, the heart and blood vessels do less work to maintain blood pressure. Over long missions this can lead to cardiovascular deconditioning, and on return to gravity some crew members experience light-headedness when standing (orthostatic intolerance). Exercise countermeasures are used partly to limit these effects.

## Measurement context matters
Heart rate and blood pressure vary with posture, recent exercise, time of day, caffeine, stress and sleep. A single reading is difficult to interpret without context. Recording whether a heart-rate measurement was taken at rest or after exercise, and repeating an unexpected reading under standard conditions, makes comparisons with a personal baseline more meaningful.

## Personal baseline comparison
Comparing a heart rate or blood pressure measurement with the crew member's own history highlights changes that a population range might miss. A difference from baseline is a reason to repeat, track or review the measurement; it is not a diagnosis.

## Symptoms that need prompt attention
Symptoms such as chest pain, fainting, or breathlessness together with dizziness should be reported to the onboard medical officer immediately and handled through the mission's approved emergency medical procedures rather than through self-monitoring software.
