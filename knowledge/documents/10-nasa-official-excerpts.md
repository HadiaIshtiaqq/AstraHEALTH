# NASA Official Sources: Five Hazards of Human Spaceflight

> Short verbatim quotations from public NASA web pages, with source URLs, collected on 14 Nov 2026 for the
> prototype's evidence library. Text in quotation marks is NASA's; the surrounding sentences are the team's
> neutral framing. Check the live pages for the current revision before relying on them.
> Sources: NASA Human Research Program, "5 Hazards of Human Spaceflight" (https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazards/,
> page last updated 12 Jun 2026) and NASA, "The Human Body in Space"
> (https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/the-human-body-in-space/, article dated 2 Feb 2021).

## Space radiation hazard (NASA)
NASA's Human Research Program describes space radiation as invisible and "considered one of the most hazardous
aspects of spaceflight." NASA also notes that on Earth people are "shielded by the planet's magnetic field and
atmosphere from the majority of particles", and that "Health risks for astronauts from radiation exposure in space
are mainly driven by long-term impacts." NASA lists shielding, monitoring and operational procedures as ways to
protect crews. Source: nasa.gov/hrp/hazards and nasa.gov/humans-in-space/the-human-body-in-space.

## Isolation and confinement hazard (NASA)
NASA states: "Behavioral responses occur among groups of people far from Earth", when crews are isolated and
confined in a small space for a long time. NASA adds that "Expedition crews selected for a stay onboard the space
station are carefully chosen, trained, and supported", and describes attention to sleep, circadian rhythm, fatigue
and crew morale, with countermeasures such as lighting and sleep monitoring.
Source: nasa.gov/hrp/hazards and nasa.gov/humans-in-space/the-human-body-in-space.

## Distance from Earth hazard (NASA)
NASA explains that instructions, new supplies and medical care "become increasingly challenging to receive from
Earth as astronauts venture deeper into space." NASA notes that "The space station orbits 240 miles above Earth",
whereas Mars is on average about 140 million miles away, with communication delays of up to about 20 minutes one
way. Crews must therefore carry the food, equipment and medical supplies they may need and act with greater
autonomy. Source: nasa.gov/hrp/hazards and nasa.gov/humans-in-space/the-human-body-in-space.

## Gravity fields hazard (NASA)
NASA notes that the body's muscles, bones, inner ear and organs "must adjust to the new gravities encountered on
the space station or their spacecraft", and again on the Moon, on Mars and on return to Earth. NASA's Human
Research Program reports that weight-bearing bones lose on average about 1% to 1.5% of mineral density per month
during spaceflight without countermeasures, which is why exercise countermeasures matter.
Source: nasa.gov/hrp/hazards.

## Hostile and closed environments hazard (NASA)
NASA states: "Maintaining a safe ecosystem inside a spacecraft presents unique challenges", including temperature,
pressure, lighting, noise, microbes and immune responses. Environmental conditions in the habitat are monitored
and managed to protect crew health. Source: nasa.gov/hrp/hazards.

## Spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (NASA)
NASA's Human Research Program treats spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS), formerly called VIIP, as
a priority risk for long-duration missions. Reported signs include optic disc edema, chorioretinal folds, globe
flattening and a hyperopic (farsighted) shift. NASA's roadmap still lists a validated countermeasure as an open
need. Source: NASA Human Research Roadmap, Risk of SANS (https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=105).
